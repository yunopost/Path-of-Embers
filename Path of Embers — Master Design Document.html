Path of Embers

"Their lives are embers on the path to oblivion — and still they burn."
GENRE: Deckbuilding Roguelite  |  ENGINE: Godot 4.5.1  |  PLATFORM: PC · Mobile · Steam Deck  |  STATUS: Pre-Refactor  |  LAST UPDATED: 2026
Section 01

Overview & Design Pillars

Path of Embers is a party-based deckbuilding roguelite with deep meta-progression, a rich equipment system, and a unique real-time pressure mechanic. The game draws mechanical inspiration from Slay the Spire and Chaos Zero: Nightmare, while building its own identity through multi-character party construction, a somber high-fantasy aesthetic, and a narrative premise that inverts traditional heroic fantasy.

Design Pillars

Pillar 1
The Party Is the Build. No character exists in isolation. Meaningful play comes from how characters interact — through shared deck composition, complementary stats, and equipment synergies.
Pillar 2
Pressure Is Always Present. The enemy turn timer means every card played is a trade-off. Players are never simply executing an uncontested combo — they are always managing urgency.
Pillar 3
Equipment Defines Identity. A character's archetype is a starting point, not a destination. Equipment allows radical specialization and unexpected builds.
Pillar 4
Meta-Progression Has Meaning. Unlocks are tied to story. Every new path opened on the map reveals something about the world and the characters who inhabit it.
Pillar 5
The World Cannot Die. That is the horror. Art, sound, and systems should make the player feel that wrongness — something frozen mid-breath, held there forever against its will.

Tone & Feel

The world does not burn. It smolders. Everything is caught mid-moment — flowers half-bloom, rivers half-frozen, seasons that never turn. Our heroes are not conquerors. They are mercy.

The emotional register of Path of Embers is exhaustion edging toward apathy. These characters have been fighting a world that cannot end for a very long time. Hope exists but feels impossible — when it appears, it should feel like a mistake, something that slipped through. The player should never feel triumphant. The closest the game gets to relief is the prospect of a proper ending.

Three craft references, used as directives:

  • NieR: Automata — characters who understand exactly what they are sacrificing. Write dialogue that way. Nobody here is confused about the cost.
  • Shadow of the Colossus — the player does the necessary terrible thing anyway. The game does not absolve them. Neither does this one.
  • Unicorn Overlord — world-building carried in visual detail, not exposition. A soldier frozen mid-swing tells you more than a paragraph of lore.
Section 02

Narrative & World

The Premise

Before the end, there was a lich who would not let go.

The world of Path of Embers was approaching its natural conclusion — not through catastrophe, but through the slow, rightful turning of cosmic cycles. An ancient lich, having achieved true immortality, could not accept oblivion. In a desperate act of selfish preservation, he froze the world in a liminal state: between life and death, between creation and decay. Nothing grows. Nothing dies. Nothing changes.

Decades, perhaps centuries, have passed in this frozen amber. Rivers run but do not carve. Children are born but do not age. The dying linger in half-death, unable to pass on. The world's natural systems — the cycle of seasons, the rot that feeds new growth, the mercy of endings — are all suspended.

The party of heroes (and monsters, and wanderers) assembled by the player are those who have awakened to the wrongness of this stasis. Their goal is not heroic in the traditional sense: they fight to allow the world to die, because they understand that eternal stagnation is a crueler fate than any ending.

Core Themes

  • Mortality as mercy — endings are not failures; they are completions.
  • Selfishness disguised as love — the lich believes he is saving the world; he cannot see the harm in his preservation.
  • Grief and acceptance — each character carries a personal relationship with death and ending that the narrative explores.
  • Unlikely fellowship — heroes and monsters united by shared understanding, not shared nature.

Narrative Arc

Each map region is a different civilization caught mid-moment. The story unfolds through node events, environmental storytelling, and character dialogue. Meta-progression unlocks new story chapters as the player pieces together the full history of the lich's act and its consequences.

The Emotional Center of the Game
The final confrontation is not a victory. The lich is not evil — he is a being who loved the world and destroyed it trying to keep it. The player defeats him knowing he was right about one thing: the world ending is a loss. They do it anyway. That's the game.

Game Structure

Maps & Node Layout

Maps progress left to right (rather than bottom to top as in STS). Each map represents a region of the world frozen by the lich's curse. The map consists of interconnected nodes arranged in a branching path structure.

Branching Structure

Each map offers a branching network of paths from start to boss. Paths are not siloed corridors — they weave into and out of each other, sharing nodes and reconverging at junctions. A player who starts on one route can pivot to another as the map opens up. No path locks you in.

The node mix across the map is intentionally varied — some areas are denser with combat, others with events or shops — but this variation emerges from the layout rather than being labeled or sorted into explicit categories. Players read the map, make informed guesses based on visible node art and what they know about the region, and adapt when the path surprises them.

Design Guardrail
Paths must never feel like a binary choice between "combat route" and "safe route." Node variety should be distributed across the whole map so that any given path through it touches multiple node types. A player should rarely complete a map having only seen one type of node. The goal is informed agency — not predictable optimization.

Meta-Progression

Path of Embers uses a pure milestone system — there is no currency, no spend-and-upgrade tree, and no grind loop. Every milestone is a one-time event: it fires the first time its condition is met and permanently unlocks its reward. It never fires again. All progress is permanent; nothing resets. A player who has completed the game has access to everything they earned, forever.

Core Design Principle
Milestones expand what is possible — not how powerful you start. No milestone makes a character numerically stronger before a run begins. Cards and equipment enter the drop pool through play, not progression gates. Every run starts on equal mechanical footing; what changes across runs is the breadth of encounters, paths, characters, and challenges available.

Difficulty System

Before starting any standard run, players may apply difficulty modifiers from a modifier menu. Modifiers are individual toggles — players mix and match however they want. Each modifier must be unlocked through milestones before it becomes available in the menu. No modifiers are active by default; a fresh player runs at base difficulty.

Available Modifiers

The following modifiers are defined for launch. The modifier system is designed to expand — additional modifiers will be added as content is developed. Advanced Enemies (a rework of timer-based enemy difficulty) is planned but not yet specified.

Tougher Bosses All bosses have increased HP and deal increased damage Boss encounters require more complete builds to survive. Rewards players who prioritize damage and consistency over run efficiency.
Tougher Enemies All standard and elite enemies have increased HP Combats take longer, consuming more resources per fight. Efficient deck construction becomes more important.

Efficient block and damage sequencing becomes more important. Stacks with Tougher Enemies (which raises HP) for a compounding effect.

A Skirmisher that previously alternated attack/debuff may now stack attacks. Players who relied on pattern recognition must adapt to genuine uncertainty.

Enemies become qualitatively different, not just numerically harder. Requires genuine adaptation mid-combat rather than prior knowledge.

Stacking Modifiers

Multiple modifiers can be active simultaneously with no cap. Advanced Enemies tiers are cumulative — Advanced III includes all effects of I and II and cannot be selected independently. All other modifiers are independent toggles. Running more modifiers increases difficulty and increases leaderboard score weighting — see below.

Leaderboards & Difficulty

There is one leaderboard per boss or mode. All runs compete on the same board regardless of modifier configuration — difficulty is expressed as a score bonus rather than a separate category. A clean unmodified run competes directly against a fully-modified run; the modified run scores higher but both appear on the same board.

Each leaderboard tracks both personal best (displayed prominently) and full run history (accessible for players who want to track improvement over time).

Scoring Formula

Score is a composite of four weighted factors plus a modifier bonus. This is a playtesting baseline — all values subject to tuning.

ComponentFormulaWeight Rationale
Speed (primary) (10,000 ÷ seconds to clear) × 100 Dominant factor. Rewards mastery and aggressive play. A 5-minute clear scores roughly twice a 10-minute clear.
Damage dealt Total damage dealt ÷ 10 Additive bonus. Rewards builds that output high damage without penalizing survivability-focused approaches heavily.
HP remaining (HP remaining ÷ max HP pool) × 500 Proportional survival bonus. A full-HP clear adds 500 points; a 1-HP clear adds near 0. Rewards clean execution.
Efficiency 1,000 ÷ total cards played Rewards lean, efficient decks. A 50-card play run scores ~20 pts; a 20-card run scores ~50 pts. Minor factor — prevents degenerate low-card strategies from dominating but doesn't punish normal play.

Modifier Bonus

Each active modifier adds +10% of the base composite score to the final total. Advanced Enemies tiers each count as one modifier (I, II, and III are three separate modifiers for scoring purposes even though they are cumulative in effect).

Design Intent
A unified leaderboard creates a healthier competitive community than fragmented per-configuration boards. High-modifier runs naturally dominate the top but a clean fast unmodified run can still place competitively — the 60% modifier ceiling means the gap between 0 and max modifiers is meaningful but not insurmountable for skilled players. The personal best display keeps the board motivating for players at all skill levels.
Playtesting Note
All formula values are initial estimates. Speed dominance should be validated during playtesting — if fast builds trivially outpace all other factors the damage and HP components may need reweighting. The efficiency component in particular is a minor tuning lever that may be removed entirely if it creates perverse incentives.

The Boss Rush mode is a key differentiator. It is a separate mode — not accessible mid-run — where players pit a saved build from a completed standard run against a harder-than-normal version of an unlocked boss. Success grants story progression, meta-progression content, and leaderboard placement.

Boss Rush — Full Specification

Build Saving

A Boss Rush build is a complete snapshot of a party at the moment of successful run completion: the full deck (all cards and their upgrade states), all equipped equipment across all 18 slots, the run stash contents, and each character's current stat values. Builds can only be saved on successful run completion — losing runs do not generate a saveable build.

Players may have up to 3 saved builds at any time. Saving a 4th build requires overwriting one of the existing three. Saved builds are permanent until manually deleted or overwritten — they do not expire and can be used unlimited times.

Boss Availability

Boss Rush bosses unlock through story progression in standard runs — defeating a boss in a standard run, completing specific events, or discovering narrative nodes. Bosses are not available by default. The unlock system ties Boss Rush access directly to world exploration, ensuring players engage with the story before accessing the prestige mode.

Boss Rush versions of bosses are harder than their standard run counterparts — higher HP, modified move sets, additional mechanics, or increased timer pressure. They are distinct encounters, not reskins.

Rewards

Reward TypeDescription
Story & LoreDefeating a Boss Rush boss unlocks story content not available in standard runs — extended narrative scenes, world history, character backstory
Meta-ProgressionUnique equipment, character-locked items, and persistent unlocks only obtainable through Boss Rush
LeaderboardRuns are scored and ranked. Two leaderboard categories per boss: fastest clear time and highest damage dealt. Scores are tied to the saved build used, encouraging build optimization.
Design Intent
Boss Rush serves three purposes simultaneously: a prestige challenge for players who want to optimize builds beyond the standard run, a narrative reward layer that incentivizes repeated play, and a social/competitive surface through leaderboards. The 3-build save limit creates meaningful curation pressure — players choose which runs were worth preserving.
Grind Prevention
Because builds are saved only on successful run completion and Boss Rush bosses require story unlocks, access to the mode is naturally gated by legitimate play progression. Unlimited reuse of a saved build is intentional — the leaderboard scoring system rewards optimization, not volume. Running the same build 100 times doesn't inflate progression; it only improves your score.

Narrative Delivery

Path of Embers tells its story through six delivery methods operating simultaneously at different registers — from the ambient and wordless to the explicit and momentous. No single method carries the full narrative weight; they layer. The game is text-only with no voice acting, which places the full burden of tone on writing quality and visual framing.

Guiding Principle
The somber tone requires restraint. Story should feel discovered, not delivered. Players who read nothing should still understand the world through art and atmosphere. Players who read everything should feel the full weight of what the party is doing and why. Neither experience should feel incomplete.

Delivery Methods

1. Environmental Storytelling

The primary and most pervasive delivery method. The map, node location art, enemy designs, and background details tell the story without a single word of text. A frozen waterfall mid-cascade. A battlefield where soldiers stand locked in combat poses, neither side able to fall. Flowers half-open, forever. Players who never read an event node should still feel the world's wrongness through what they see.

This is the narrative baseline — it must work on its own before any text layer is added.

2. Pre/Post Combat Dialogue

Short character exchanges — 1 to 4 lines — that fire before or after specific combat encounters. These are not mandatory story beats; they are texture. A Warrior noting something grim about the enemy. Two characters disagreeing about whether what they're doing is right. An enemy's final words. Kept short deliberately — a 4-line exchange that lands is worth more than a paragraph that overstays its welcome.

All pre/post combat dialogue is skippable after first viewing. On repeat runs it auto-advances after a brief delay or on any input.

3. Event Nodes

Text-based choice screens at Event map nodes. The primary interactive narrative moment. An event presents a situation — environmental, social, moral — and offers 2 to 4 choices with visible or hidden consequences. Events are where the world's specific details live: named locations, named NPCs, the particular texture of what stasis has done to this region.

Events should read fast. Target length: scene-setting in 3 to 5 sentences, choice options in one sentence each. Players are making run decisions at event nodes, not reading a novel.

4. Cutscenes

Dedicated narrative scenes reserved for key story moments only — milestone unlocks, boss defeats, major story revelations. Rendered as illustrated panels with text overlays, not animated sequences. Cutscenes are the only mandatory narrative moments on first viewing; all others can be skipped. On repeat runs, all cutscenes are skippable from the first frame.

Cutscenes should be used sparingly. If a moment can be told through a post-combat exchange or an event node, it should be. Cutscenes are for moments the game needs to stop and breathe.

5. Combat Dialogue

Brief lines from party members and enemies that fire during combat — on specific card plays, when HP drops below a threshold, when an enemy uses a special mechanic, or on kill. Always short: one line, no more. Combat dialogue runs beneath the mechanical layer without interrupting it. Players focused on the timer system will hear it as atmosphere; players paying attention will pick up characterization and lore fragments.

Combat dialogue is never story-critical. Nothing that appears only in combat dialogue should be required to understand the narrative.

6. Journal / Codex

An optional repository of lore entries, character notes, enemy descriptions, and world history — unlocked progressively through milestones, event outcomes, and enemy first encounters. Never surfaced intrusively; the player opens it by choice. The journal is for players who want to go deeper. It should reward that curiosity with genuine substance — history of the world before the lich's act, the specific nature of the stasis in different regions, character backstory that doesn't fit naturally into dialogue.

Journal entries are written in an in-world voice, not a game manual voice.

Character Dialogue

Party members speak to each other and to the world — they are not silent protagonists. Each character has a distinct voice shaped by their archetype, background, and personal relationship with the party's mission. Characters may disagree with each other, express doubt, find dark humor, or sit in silence when silence is more honest than words.

Writing Guardrails
No exposition dumps. Characters do not explain the world to each other — they already know it. Lore comes from context, not lectures.

No false hope. The party knows what they are fighting for. Dialogue should not pretend otherwise or reach for unearned optimism.

Write plainly. Avoid em dashes, rhetorical questions, and sentences that reach for poetry before they earn it. The emotional register is exhaustion near apathy — ornate language is wrong for this world.

Wrong: “The world cries out for release — a mercy only you can give.”
Right: “Nothing here has ended in a long time.”

Enemies have voices too. Pre-combat and death lines for enemies should reflect the world's stasis — many of them have been frozen in this half-life for a long time and they know it.

Section 04

Characters & Party System

Starting Roster

Three characters are available at game start — one per archetype. All remaining characters unlock through milestone progression. The starting three are chosen to give new players immediate access to all three archetype play styles and to teach core systems through direct mechanical expression.

CharacterArchetypeWhy They Start Unlocked
Monster Hunter Warrior His kit directly engages with the timer system — the game's most distinctive mechanic. Full Attack (Slow) and Shoulder Tackle (Haste Next Card) teach timer advancement and manipulation from the first combat. New players learn the system by playing the character most explicitly built around it.
Witch Healer Immediately communicates that Healers are not a traditional support class. Curse generation and Malediction Lash establish the archetype's debuff/control identity from the first hand. Her cards are mechanically clear despite being unconventional — a good first lesson about the archetype's breadth.
Living Armor Defender The most intuitive Defender fantasy: build Block, convert it to damage. Plated Guard and Iron Tide together teach Block accumulation and offensive conversion without requiring complex system knowledge. The defense-into-offense loop is readable on first play.
New Player Onboarding Note
The starting party of Monster Hunter + Witch + Living Armor gives a new player all three archetypes but no obvious "safe" choice — none of the three are conventional archetypes. This is intentional. Path of Embers should establish early that its characters are not genre defaults. The character selection screen must carry enough descriptive weight that a new player can evaluate all three without prior knowledge of the game.

Character Quests

Each character has a pool of 4–5 quests. At the start of each run, one quest is drawn randomly from each party member's pool and assigned to them for that run. Quests are run-scoped — they reset every run. There is no permanent completion state.

Final Boss Gate
The final boss is locked until all three party members' quests have been completed within the same run. All three must be complete by the time the party reaches the end of Map 3. A run where even one quest is incomplete cannot access the final encounter that run. This makes party selection a dual decision: card synergy and quest co-completion feasibility.

Quest Completion Rewards

Completing a quest mid-run grants an immediate bonus reward defined by that specific quest — not a flat reward. Each quest in a character's pool has its own reward attached. Harder quests carry better rewards, creating a risk/reward layer on top of the randomized assignment. Reward types include gold, upgrade points, cards, equipment, or transcendence — defined per quest during content design.

Quest Design Guidelines

  • Character-specific — conditions should feel native to that character's mechanics. Tempest's quests involve high card volume; Sibyl's involve Scry and deck ordering; Hollow's involve Block conversion.
  • Achievable in one run but not passively — quests should require deliberate play, not just happen by accident.
  • Legible at run start — players should immediately understand what kind of play a quest demands when they see it assigned.
  • Varied difficulty within a pool — each character's 4–5 quests should include easier and harder conditions so that any given run's random draw has a difficulty spread.
  • Reward proportional to difficulty — harder quest conditions attach better rewards.

Players select 3 characters before each run. Party composition choices are meaningful from card one — the ratio of Warriors to Healers to Defenders directly shapes the deck's early-game profile and upgrade priorities.

HP Pool Construction
The party's total HP at the start of a run is the sum of all three characters' HP values (Warrior 25, Defender 28, Healer 22). A balanced party of one each starts at exactly 75 HP. Equipment that raises a character's HP stat directly increases the shared pool — a Chest piece granting +20 HP adds 20 to the pool regardless of which character equips it.

Card System

Cards are the primary combat resource. The combined party deck is shuffled and drawn from as a single shared pool during combat — but each card is owned by a specific character and scales only with that character's stats.

Card Ownership

Cards are character-tagged. A Monster Hunter's Strike card deals damage scaled by the Monster Hunter's STR — not the party's average, not another character's STR, not any shared value. Equipping another character with high STR has no effect on a card that doesn't belong to them.

Why This Matters
Card ownership makes stat investment meaningful and targeted. Stacking STR on a Warrior via equipment is a direct amplifier for that Warrior's cards specifically. It also means two parties with the same cards but different characters equipped play fundamentally differently, even mid-run.

Rare Card Pity System

Rare card appearance is governed by a dynamic probability counter rather than a fixed drop rate:

  • Base rare chance starts at -2% (effectively 0%).
  • Each time a Common card appears in a reward screen, the rare chance increases by +1%.
  • When a Rare card appears, the counter resets to -2%.
  • Elite nodes grant a flat +10% bonus to the current rare counter.
  • Each copy of a specific Rare card already in the deck applies a -10% penalty to that card appearing again — preventing rare stacking without blocking it entirely.
Strategic Implication
Elite nodes are the most reliable source of Rare cards due to the +10% bonus. Seeing Commons builds pity toward a future Rare. Owning a Rare makes finding a second copy significantly harder, which naturally diversifies rare card acquisition across a run.

Transcendence

The lich believed immortality was the final form of anything that mattered. He was wrong about everything except the transformation.

Transcendence is a rare, powerful transformation of a card — inspired by CZN's transcend mechanic. When a card transcends, it can undergo one of several types of radical change:

  • Power Elevation — A Skill or Attack becomes a Power (ongoing passive effect).
  • Fission — The card splits into two cards, each containing an upgraded portion of the original's effects.
  • Identity Shift — The card transforms into a fundamentally different card sharing thematic DNA but with a new mechanical identity.
  • Apotheosis — The card becomes an entirely new, unique card not available through any other means.

Equipment System

Equipment occupies the design space of relics in STS but is character-bound and more granular. Equipment is the rarest and most impactful reward type in the game, equivalent in weight to a relic drop but with additional mechanical integration.

What Equipment Does

  • Stat Modification — Equipment modifies STR, DEF, SPIRIT, or HP on the equipped character.
  • Card Injection — Some equipment adds specific cards to the equipped character's card pool at the start of each run (or combat).
  • Combat Abilities — Some equipment grants active abilities usable during combat, outside the normal card play structure.
  • Map Abilities — Some equipment grants abilities usable on the map (e.g., reveal hidden nodes, alter event outcomes).

Equipment Slots

Each character has 6 typed equipment slots. Slots are typed — only equipment of the matching slot type can be equipped in that slot. Equipment is universally equippable with no archetype or character restrictions on the base slot system; however, individual items may carry archetype locks or character locks as part of their design (see Equip Restrictions below).

The Relic slot is intentionally separated from the gear slots. Where Helmet through Weapon are about making a character stronger, the Relic slot is about making a character different. Combat System

Combat in Path of Embers is turn-based but not passive. The Enemy Turn Timer System replaces traditional fixed enemy turns with a dynamic countdown model driven entirely by player actions. Every card played is a decision about time — not just effect.

Enemy Turn Timer System

Time doesn't move here on its own. You move it. That's the cost.

Core Rules

Rule 1 — Enemy Timers
Each enemy has a visible countdown timer representing how many ticks remain before they act. When the timer reaches 0, the enemy immediately performs its current intent. After acting, the enemy selects its next move and resets its timer to that move's defined value.
Rule 2 — Playing Cards Advances Time
Time does not pass automatically. Each card played advances all enemy timers by 1 tick (default). Every action the player takes brings all enemies closer to acting.
Rule 3 — End Turn Forces Action
Ending the player's turn forces all enemy timers to 0 immediately. Every enemy acts — regardless of where their individual timers were. This is the critical tradeoff at the heart of the system: play more cards for more value, or end turn early to trigger enemy actions under controlled conditions.

Timer Manipulation Keywords

Two keywords directly modify timer advancement. Full keyword definitions are in §05 — the authoritative glossary.

  • Haste — playing this card advances enemy timers by 0 ticks instead of 1. High-tempo play without time cost; these cards tend to have reduced raw power to compensate.
  • Slow — playing this card advances enemy timers by 2 ticks instead of 1. High-power, high-risk. A Slow card played at the wrong moment can trigger multiple simultaneous enemy actions.
  • Haste Next Card — a status effect applied by certain cards; the next card played this turn has Haste.

Strategic Implications

Timing Is a Resource. Players must decide not just what to play, but when to play it — and whether to delay, force, or sequence around enemy actions.

Order Matters. The same hand played in a different sequence produces different outcomes. Playing block before a timer triggers is correct play; playing block after is a waste. Card sequencing is a core skill layer.

Controlled Enemy Turns. Players can intentionally trigger enemy attacks while protected (e.g., with block pre-applied), delay dangerous moves until counters are in place, or force multiple enemies to act simultaneously when that's tactically advantageous.

Multi-Enemy Complexity. Each enemy maintains an independent timer. This creates overlapping action windows, priority decisions about which enemy to accelerate or delay, and situations where multiple enemies act in the same tick. When multiple enemies reach 0 simultaneously — whether mid-turn or on end turn — they resolve left to right. This means block played before a multi-enemy window protects against the leftmost attacker first; surviving hits from the right side is the player's sequencing responsibility.

Monster Hunter directly manipulates timers as a core identity — expect cards that reduce, freeze, or reset specific enemy timers. Shadowfoot's fast action / combo chain identity maps naturally to Haste — high volume at low timer cost. Hollow's end-turn effects synergize with deliberately triggering the end-turn force, using it as a resource rather than a concession. Tempest's random effects must never produce unpredictable timer behavior — randomness is in outcomes, not tick advancement.

Energy System

Each turn the player starts with 3 energy. Cards cost energy to play. Unspent energy does not carry over — energy refills fully at the start of each turn. Cards with a cost of 0 are free to play but still advance enemy timers by 1 unless they have Haste.

Base energy can be modified by equipment and Power cards. Energy modification is intentionally rare in the equipment pool and should come with meaningful trade-offs when it appears — an item that grants +1 base energy is one of the most powerful things in the game and should be priced and weighted accordingly. Power cards that modify energy similarly represent a high-value build axis. Negative energy modification (starting with less than 3) may also appear as a drawback on otherwise powerful equipment.

Hand Management

At the start of each turn, the player draws 5 cards. Unplayed cards are discarded at end of turn. When the draw pile is empty, the discard pile is shuffled to form a new draw pile. There is no hand size limit during the play phase.

Block

Block is a single additive value that absorbs incoming damage before it reaches the shared HP pool. The rules below are the default for all Block in the game — any card or effect that behaves differently will state so explicitly (e.g. Living Armor's Plated Guard).

RuleBehavior
Stacking Block is additive. Playing multiple Block cards in a turn adds their values together into a single pool.
Damage absorption Incoming damage hits Block first. If damage exceeds current Block, the excess carries through to the HP pool. Block is reduced to 0 in the process — it is never reduced below 0.
Decay All Block is removed at the start of the player's turn. Block does not persist between turns by default.
Persistent Block Some cards and effects explicitly prevent Block from decaying. Persistent Block carries into the next turn and stacks additively with any new Block gained that turn — it is not tracked separately. The only difference is it survives the start-of-turn decay that would normally remove it.
Design Note — Timing with the Timer System
Because enemies can act mid-turn (when their timer hits 0), Block gained earlier in a turn protects against those mid-turn actions. Block gained after an enemy has already acted does not retroactively absorb that damage. Sequencing Block cards before anticipated enemy actions is a core skill expression — the timer system makes Block timing a real decision in a way that purely turn-based systems do not.

Combat Flow

  1. Draw Phase — Player draws 5 cards. Energy refills to 3.
  2. Play Phase — Player plays cards in chosen sequence, spending energy. Each card play advances all enemy timers by 1 (or 0 for Haste / 2 for Slow). When any enemy's timer reaches 0, that enemy acts immediately and resets.
  3. End Turn — Player passes. All remaining enemy timers are forced to 0; all enemies act left to right. Hand is discarded.
  4. Repeat until combat ends.

Multi-Character Combat

All three party members share a single combined HP pool. There is no per-character HP tracking — the party lives and dies as a unit. When the shared HP pool reaches 0, the run ends. No character can be individually incapacitated or removed from play during combat.

Design Implication
Because HP is pooled, all character cards remain fully functional for the entire combat regardless of how much damage the party has taken. A character's STR, DEF, and SPIRIT values always apply at full strength to their owned cards. There are no dead-weight cards, no degraded scaling states, and no mid-combat roster management. The only question is whether the pool reaches 0.
Tone Note
A shared HP pool reinforces the thematic core. They endure together, or not at all.
Section 07a

Run End State

Successful Run Completion

When the final boss is defeated, the run ends in success. All equipment found during the run transfers to the persistent stash in full. Any active milestones that haven't yet fired are checked and resolved. Boss Rush save slot becomes available.

Death — HP Pool Reaches 0

When the shared HP pool reaches 0 the screen fades to black. A contextual death message is displayed — drawn from a pool of possible messages filtered by two parameters:

  • Map tier — which map the party was on when they died (Map 1, 2, or 3)
  • Cause of death — which enemy or boss delivered the killing blow

Messages are written in the game's voice — somber, understated, in keeping with the world's tone. Dying to a common enemy on Map 1 reads differently than dying to the final boss on Map 3. The message pool is a content design task; volume and variety should be sufficient that repeat playthroughs rarely see the same message twice in the same context.

Loot Retention on Death

On a failed run, the player keeps a portion of equipment found during that run. The retention rate scales with the number of active difficulty modifiers — harder configurations are more forgiving on failure.

Active ModifiersRetention RateExample — 10 items found
0 (base difficulty)20%Keep 2 items
1~27%Keep ~3 items
2~33%Keep ~3 items
340%Keep 4 items
4~47%Keep ~5 items
5~53%Keep ~5 items

The player chooses which items to keep — retention is not random. After the death screen, the player is shown all equipment found during the run and selects up to the retention limit. Unchosen items are lost. Chosen items transfer to the persistent stash.

Design Intent — Retention Scaling
Harder runs losing less on death serves two purposes. It compensates players who take on greater risk — a failed run at max modifiers still yields meaningful persistent progress. It also creates a natural incentive to run higher difficulty configurations even for players who don't expect to win, which keeps the modifier system engaged beyond the competitive leaderboard context.
Rounding
Retention rates that produce fractional item counts round down. A player who found 5 items at base difficulty (20% = 1.0) keeps 1 item. A player who found 7 items at base difficulty (20% = 1.4) keeps 1 item. Rounding direction and fractional handling should be confirmed during implementation.

Milestone Behavior on Death

Milestones trigger immediately when their condition is met — not at run end. A player who deals 100+ damage in a single turn and then dies on the next combat still receives that milestone unlock. Failed runs contribute fully to milestone progress. There is no penalty to milestone progression for dying.

Section 07b

Enemy Design Philosophy

Enemies in Path of Embers are not just damage sources — they are the primary mechanism through which the timer system creates meaningful decisions. Enemy design must always serve the timer: how an enemy's archetype, timer value, and special mechanics interact with the player's card sequence is the central design question for every encounter.

Regional Rosters

Each map has an entirely unique enemy roster — enemies do not carry over between regions. This ensures each map feels distinct in its threat profile and demands different strategic responses from the player. The number of distinct regional factions is TBD and left for content design, but the principle of full roster separation per map is fixed.

Enemy Archetypes

All enemies belong to one of five archetypes. Archetypes define timer range and base behavioral role. Within an archetype, individual enemies differentiate through move sets, special mechanics, and regional theming.

ArchetypeTimer RangeHP ProfileRoleDesign Intent
Skirmisher 1–2 Low Frequent attacker Creates constant timer pressure. Punishes high-volume card play hard — nearly every card played triggers a Skirmisher action. Priority kill target in multi-enemy encounters.
Heavy 4–6 High Slow, punishing hitter Allows the player to play several cards before acting. Hits very hard when it does act. Creates a countdown tension — the player knows a big hit is coming and must prepare.
Support 2–4 Medium Ally buffer / shielder Rarely a direct threat to the player. Dangerous because it amplifies other enemies. Priority kill target when paired with Heavies or Summoners. Tests target priority decisions.
Summoner 4–5 Medium–High Spawns additional enemies Slow enough that the player has time to react, but spawned enemies immediately enter the timer system with their own countdown. Ignored Summoners compound the board state rapidly.
Disruptor 2–4 Low–Medium Timer and card manipulation The most mechanically complex archetype. Can advance player card tick costs, reset enemy timers to full, or interfere with the hand. Directly attacks the system the player is trying to control.

Special Enemy Mechanics

Beyond archetype behavior, individual enemies can carry special mechanics as properties. These are not archetype-locked — any archetype can have any property, though some pairings are more natural than others.

MechanicEffectNatural Archetype Fit
Shield Absorbs damage up to a threshold before HP is affected. Shield may regenerate on the enemy's action. Heavy, Support
Timer Manipulation On action, alters timer progression — can advance all enemy timers (accelerating the board), reset its own timer to full (delaying itself), or add ticks to the player's next card play cost. Disruptor
Summon Spawns one or more additional enemies mid-combat. Spawned enemies enter with their own timer already counting. Summoner
Ally Buff On action, applies a buff to one or all other enemies — increased damage, reduced timer, or temporary shield. Support
Flip While this enemy is alive, healing effects deal damage to the party instead and damage-dealing cards restore HP to enemies. Applies to all sources — cards, equipment abilities, passive effects. Any — used sparingly as a high-impact property
Flip — Design Notes
The Flip mechanic is the most disruptive property in the game. It inverts the function of Healer characters entirely and turns aggressive cards into a liability if the player isn't careful. Flip enemies should be: visually distinct at a glance, never paired with Skirmishers (the player needs time to adapt), and used rarely enough that encountering one is a notable event. Flip is a property, not an archetype — it should appear on specific named enemies, not as a generic modifier.

Multi-Enemy Encounters

Combat encounters can feature multiple enemies simultaneously, each with independent timers. Multi-enemy design should use archetype combinations deliberately to create distinct threat profiles:

  • Skirmisher + Heavy — constant pressure from the Skirmisher while the Heavy charges a devastating hit. Classic tension pairing.
  • Summoner + Support — left uncontrolled, the board floods with shielded enemies. Tests prioritization hard.
  • Disruptor + Heavy — the Disruptor advances timers or adds tick costs while the Heavy charges. The Heavy hits before the player is ready.
  • Support + two Skirmishers — buffed fast attackers. Block efficiency is critical; the Support must die first.
Design Guardrail
No encounter should feel like it requires a specific party composition to survive — only a specific strategic response. Encounters test decision-making within the timer system, not deck gatekeeping. If a Flip enemy makes a Healer-heavy party unwinnable rather than just harder, that's a design failure.

Enemy AI

Enemies select moves using weighted random selection with anti-repetition rules to prevent predictable patterns while allowing some natural repetition:

  • An enemy will never use the same move 3 times in a row.
  • If an enemy uses the same move 2 times in a row, that move's weight is reduced by 50% on the next selection.

Each move has its own timer value. After an enemy acts, its timer resets to the timer value of its newly selected next move — meaning the same enemy can have different tick counts between actions depending on which move it selects.

Example Enemy — Ash Men (Act 1)
HP: 38–44 (randomized)

Move 1 (Timer 4): Deal 8 damage
Move 2 (Timer 1): Apply Vulnerable 1 to party
Move 3 (Timer 4): Deal 2 damage, heal 2 HP
Move 4 (Timer 3): Deal 3 damage × 2 hits

Note how Move 2 at Timer 1 means if the Ash Men selects it, the player has only 1 card play before Vulnerable lands — a very different threat profile from its Timer 4 moves.
Section 08

Art Direction

The visual style of Path of Embers is inspired by ATLUS's Unicorn Overlord and Ogre Battle 64: painterly unit sprites, richly detailed environments, a warm-but-muted color palette, and a strong sense of worldbuilding embedded in visual design.

Key Visual Principles

  • Stasis Made Beautiful — The world is frozen, but it should not look ugly. Ice crystals in rivers, mid-fall leaves suspended, half-bloomed flowers. The tragedy is that it's gorgeous.
  • Warm Decay — Color palette leans amber, ash, deep green, faded gold. Not grimdark gray — more like old paintings left in the sun.
  • Character Expressiveness — Characters should read emotionally at small sizes. Silhouettes are important. Unit sprites convey personality through posture, not just design.
  • Map as World — The map screen is not an abstraction. It should feel like looking at a world, with weather, time of day implied, and nodes rendered as real locations.

Animation Philosophy

Combat animations should be punchy and readable — prioritizing clarity over spectacle. Each attack should feel impactful. Card plays should have satisfying, snappy feedback. The enemy timer triggering mid-turn should have a distinct visual and audio cue that communicates urgency without panic.

Contextual death messages filtered by map tier and cause of death are defined as a system. The actual message content is a writing task. Volume should be sufficient that repeat playthroughs rarely see the same message in the same context.
📋 Story Node Content
Story nodes spawn on condition and deliver fixed rewards. The specific nodes, their unlock conditions, their narrative content, and their rewards are all content design work tied to the broader narrative arc.
📋 Equipment Pool
The equipment slot system, rarity tiers, and pricing are fully defined. The actual equipment items — their names, stat values, effects, and rarity assignments — are content production work. Character-locked items (2–4 per character) need individual design.
📋 Event Pool
Event nodes are defined as text-based choice screens with variable rewards. The actual event content — scenarios, choice options, outcomes, and rewards — is a writing and design task.
